# Needs Assessment Framework

Before designing a single training session, you need to know three things: **who** you're training, **what they already know (or think they know)**, and **how ready the organization actually is**. Skipping this step is the single most common reason AI enablement programs train the wrong content at the wrong depth.

## Step 1 — Stakeholder Mapping

Group your audience into three bands. Don't design one curriculum for all three — their needs, fears, and use cases are different.

| Group | Typical concerns | What they need first |
|---|---|---|
| **Leadership / decision-makers** | Risk, cost, liability, competitive advantage | A strategy-level briefing: what AI can/can't do, governance implications, adoption roadmap |
| **Practitioners / staff** | "Will this replace me?", time pressure, fear of doing it wrong | Hands-on, low-stakes practice tied directly to their existing daily tasks |
| **Power users / future champions** | Wanting to go deeper, becoming the go-to person | Advanced technique + a coaching role, not just more content |

## Step 2 — Skill & Sentiment Survey

A short, anonymous survey run *before* any training. Keep it under 10 questions. Sample items:

1. How often do you currently use any AI tool for work tasks? (Never / Rarely / Weekly / Daily)
2. Rate your comfort writing an effective prompt from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very comfortable).
3. What's your biggest concern about using AI in your role? (open text)
4. Which task would you most want AI to help with, if you trusted it? (open text)
5. Have you had any formal training on AI tools before? (Yes/No — if yes, what?)

The open-text questions matter more than the scale questions — they tell you what language and examples to use in session one.

## Step 3 — Current-State AI Usage Audit

Before you introduce anything new, find out what's already happening informally. This usually surfaces two things: shadow use of consumer AI tools with no safety guardrails, and pockets of genuine expertise you can recruit as champions later.

- What tools are staff already using, sanctioned or not?
- Where is sensitive data at risk of being pasted into a public tool?
- Who's already experimenting well, unprompted?

## Step 4 — Readiness Maturity Rubric

Score the organization (or each department) against this 5-level scale. This becomes your baseline to measure progress against later.

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Unaware | Little to no exposure; AI is an abstract concept, not a tool |
| 2. Aware | Staff know AI tools exist but haven't used them for work |
| 3. Experimenting | Informal, inconsistent use; no shared standards or safety practice |
| 4. Adopting | Structured training has started; consistent basic use across teams |
| 5. Embedded | AI use is standard practice, safely governed, with internal champions supporting others |

## Output: Readiness Snapshot

Summarize Steps 1–4 into a single one-page snapshot before you design the curriculum. This is what you bring to leadership to justify the program, and what you revisit in 6 months to show impact.

```
Readiness Snapshot — [Organization/Department]
Date:
Overall maturity level: [1-5]
Leadership priority: [their stated top concern]
Practitioner top concern: [most common survey theme]
Shadow AI use detected: [Y/N — where]
Recommended starting tier: [Foundations / Practitioner / Advanced]
```
